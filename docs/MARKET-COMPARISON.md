**How to read this.** Every fact about another game comes from a page fetched for this note (see Sources). Where I could not confirm something, it says **unverified**. Ratings: PEGI is the UK/European age rating, ESRB the US one, Apple's is the App Store age, and "CSM" is Common Sense Media's recommended age. ## 3. What these games do better than Full Plate, for a child

**Fun in the first minute.** Papa's, Cooking Fever and Good Pizza all put food under her fingers in the first few seconds. Full Plate opens with planning: menu, hiring, stock. Even on Cosy, a child spends her first minutes on spreadsheet-shaped screens before anything moves. The hands-on shift is Full Plate's best answer, but it sits behind the career start or a "Jump in" button.

**Instant feedback, a hundred times a session.** Cooking Mama gives up to three stars for *every step*. Papa's scores every station. Overcooked shows the order ticket burning down. Full Plate's richest feedback comes once a day, in the evening results, with a three-star score card after a hands-on shift. During the owner's service she mostly watches.

**Juice.** Papa's and Good Pizza have constant small sounds, popping coins and customer reactions. Full Plate deliberately keeps things quiet: a few soft synth blips, confetti only on the win screen and a gentle count-up of the night's takings. That is calm and suits reduced motion. Next to these games, though, it will feel muted to a 10-year-old.

**Characters.** Good Pizza has more than 80 named customers with personalities and a rival who taunts you. Papa's customers have names, level up and come back. Dreamlight Valley gives you Disney friends. Full Plate's staff do have names, faces, likes, dislikes and quotes, which is good. Its *customers*, though, are types (students, families, foodies), and there is no mascot, even though the brief asked for "memorable customers and a mascot".

**Progression she can see.** Papa's unlocks a new topping or customer every rank. Cooking Mama: Cuisine fills a recipe book. Animal Crossing's new hotel pays tickets for decorating. Full Plate has goals with reward chips and a decorating tab. Its big progress steps (the fork, franchises, stars) are hours apart: 1.8 hours at best to reach the fork, and realistically 4.5 to 9 hours for a child, per the red team.

**Collectables.** Almost every game here has something to fill: a recipe book, customer badges, outfits, furniture. Full Plate has 45 decor items and 71 dishes but no "collection" screen that shows what she has found and what is still missing.

**Playing together.** Overcooked is the family game for this age. Full Plate is single-player. The brief listed "a parent-and-child co-op option", but it has not been built.

## 5. Where Full Plate is already ahead for a parent

- **Nothing to buy, ever.** Cooking Fever sells packs up to £26.49 and carries Apple's "Loot Boxes" label. Diner DASH Adventures is rated 13+ for loot boxes and contests. Pokémon Café ReMix sells random items. Roblox games run on Robux. Even the gentle Toca Boca World has a shop. Full Plate has no ads and no purchases. The only cash anywhere is in-game money.
- **No strangers.** Roblox now needs a face age check before chat. A 9–12-year-old can still chat with anyone from under-9 to 15. Full Plate has no chat and no other players, and the only account is the parent's.
- **No grown-up drinks by default.** Chef Life carries an ESRB "Alcohol Reference" for wine and whiskey in its recipes. Full Plate has seven alcoholic drinks in its data, all hidden behind a setting that starts switched on.
- **Kind words.** The red team read all 892 lines of game text and found no insults, shaming or violence. CSM flags mean customers in Good Pizza and a "stern taskmaster" in Cooking Mama.
- **Fair progress.** There are no timers to wait out, no energy bars and no "wait or pay real money" (CSM's verdict on Cooking Fever). The simulation is deterministic: nothing is random, so nothing pushes her to pay for another try.
- **It teaches something real.** Good Pizza earns CSM's praise for showing ingredient costs. Full Plate goes further: rent, wages, a loan, menu choices, staff wellbeing and two honest ways to grow, all explained in plain words with tips on tap.
- **Built for her eyes and hands.** Tap targets are at least 44 px, no text is smaller than 13 px, there is a Bigger-text setting, motion respects reduced-motion settings, and it works in both tablet orientations. Most mobile games here are not built to these standards (unverified for each one).
- **Saves follow her.** There are named save slots and an account copy that works on any device, and it keeps playing offline after a reload. Common Sense parent reviews of Cooking Fever mention lost progress, and the Toca Boca World review notes unreliable restores across devices.
- **No privacy leaks.** The red team found zero third-party requests: no analytics, no ad networks. Cooking Fever and Toca Boca World both list data used for advertising or marketing in their store privacy labels.
- **Free and lasting.** It costs nothing and offers 10 to 20 hours per run with two different endings. Overcooked costs £8 to £30, and Apple Arcade costs £6.99 a month.

**Fair warning.** The red team review of 10 November listed some child-facing problems:

- Losing is quiet and permanent: the game-over screen deletes the save.
- A Cosy player with no staff can get stuck at £0.
- Some trade words remain on key screens.

Some of these may have been fixed since that review. Check them before her next playtest. A parent will forgive missing juice, but not a lost restaurant.

# Full Plate and the restaurant management games on the market

*Market comparison, 10 November 2026. This covers games where running the business is the point (menus, staff, money, growth). The quick-reaction cooking games for children (Overcooked, Cooking Mama, Papa's, Cooking Fever, Toca Boca, Diner Dash and similar) are covered in a separate note.*

**How to read this.** Every fact about another game comes from a page fetched for this note and listed under Sources. Steam's own pages showed US dollar prices, so a price is in pounds only where a UK store or UK price tracker confirmed it. Anything I could not confirm is marked *unverified*. The "depth of management" score (1 to 5) is my own judgement from the features each store page lists, not a published figure. Most PC-only Steam games have no PEGI or ESRB rating at all; "none listed" means the store page showed no rating.

## 3. Where Full Plate stands

### Where it is ahead

- **A real ending, and a choice of two.** Of the games checked, only Restaurant Empire II and Chef Life give a clear end goal (missions; a Michelin star). PlateUp! and Restaurant Tycoon 2 have none. Full Plate offers two that feel different: be everywhere, or be the best.
- **Franchising.** No game I checked models franchising. Restaurant Empire II has four cities and buying out rivals; GastroCity and Robot In Kitchen open more branches; Big Ambitions covers a whole city's businesses. None mentions franchise partners or royalties.
- **Starting as an employee.** None of the pages describes beginning as a junior cook or waiter and working up to owning the place. Cooking Simulator's career builds a reputation, but you are already the chef.
- **Staff who feel like people, without being harsh.** Recipe for Disaster has the closest system (traits, mood, stamina, walk-outs), but it is written for adults and players rate it only 69% positive. Full Plate's likes, dislikes, friendships and "why they feel like that" lines go further, in words a child can read.
- **Free, with nothing to buy.** The free rivals both make money from children: Good Pizza, Great Pizza carries ads and sells gems; Restaurant Tycoon 2 lists 11 game passes and 41 paid items, and the guide I read says passes speed up things like auto-cooking. Chef Life on Switch carries PEGI's "In-game purchases" label. Full Plate has no ads and nothing for sale.
- **Saves that follow her.** No store page claimed saves that move between devices or a save file you can keep. Full Plate has six slots, account sync and a file to download.
- **Tablet first.** Every deep management game here is Windows or desktop only. The ones on a handheld (PlateUp!, Chef Life) are on Switch, and touch support is not stated.

### Where it is behind

- **Amount of stuff.** Restaurant Empire II has 600+ recipes and 1,400 decor items; Tavern Keeper has "thousands" of items and a workshop for making furniture; Chef has 350+ decor pieces you can place freely. Full Plate has 9 decor slots and 45 items. For a child who loves decorating, this is the most visible gap.
- **Free placement.** In Tavern Keeper, Tavern Master, Chef, PlateUp! and Restaurant Tycoon 2 you put things exactly where you want. Full Plate picks one item per slot.
- **Inventing recipes.** Chef and Recipe for Disaster both have recipe editors. Full Plate picks dishes from a list.
- **Playing together.** PlateUp! (1–4 players, local and online), Cook, Serve, Delicious! 3 (split-screen) and Cooking Simulator 2 all have co-op, and Restaurant Tycoon 2 lets you visit friends' restaurants. Full Plate is single-player; a parent-and-child mode is only an idea in the brief.
- **Story and characters.** Tavern Keeper has nearly 100 stories and a narrated storybook; Good Pizza, Great Pizza has 80+ customers with their own personalities and a rival called Alicante. Full Plate's customers are mostly crowds, and it has no named rival.
- **Time to the fork.** The red-team review found the first year (when the paths split) takes a child roughly 4.5 to 9 hours. That is long next to PlateUp!'s runs or a Good Pizza, Great Pizza day. A first-year shortcut is on the to-do list.
- **Upright tablet.** The red-team review found an iPad held upright scrolls sideways. On master today the layout still has a 1,024-pixel minimum width, so treat portrait play as not yet working.
- **Polish and track record.** Every rival has thousands of player reviews; Full Plate has one tester.

### Ideas worth borrowing

1. **A named rival restaurant across the road** (*Good Pizza, Great Pizza*: "compete against your pizza rival, Alicante"). A ten-year-old understands "beat the shop over the road" straight away. One cartoon rival with a face and a weekly score is easier to follow than a chart of market share.
2. **Regular customers with names and favourite orders** (*Good Pizza, Great Pizza*: 80+ customers with their own orders and personalities). Pillar 2 of the brief says the customer is always visible. Five or six regulars who come back, comment and react when the menu changes would give her someone to cook *for*.
3. **"Turn this place around" challenges** (*Recipe for Disaster*: scenarios where you rescue a failing restaurant). Short, 20-minute puzzles with a clear goal ("get to 4 stars by Friday") suit a child's play sessions far better than one long year, and they teach one idea at a time.
4. **A star rating she can break down** (*Restaurant Tycoon 2*: one 1–5 star score built from cleanliness, satisfaction, food and speed). Full Plate's popularity-and-prestige numbers are accurate but abstract. A single star rating with four coloured parts is what Roblox players already know.
5. **Rooms and items with their own star rating** (*Tavern Master*: overnight rooms rated 1 to 5 stars). Giving each part of the restaurant (dining room, kitchen, toilets, outside) its own small star score gives decorating a goal, not just a look.
6. **Make-your-own decor** (*Tavern Keeper*'s item workshop; *Chef*'s individually coloured pieces). Even a simple "choose the colour and pattern" on the existing 45 items would multiply the choice without new artwork, and self-expression is what this age group asks for most.
7. **A food-truck or market-stall chapter** (*Cook, Serve, Delicious! 3*: upgrade a truck with modules and 100+ trinkets). A small mobile kitchen is cheaper and quicker than a restaurant, so it is a natural Cosy first week before the lease and loan.
8. **Visit a friend's restaurant** (*Restaurant Tycoon 2*: try friends' menus). The brief already wants a parent-and-child mode. A read-only "visit Dad's restaurant" built on the existing account saves would be a first step that needs no live multiplayer.

## 4. What a parent would compare it to

A parent looking at Full Plate will probably hold it up against three things. The first is **Restaurant Tycoon 2 on Roblox**, because that is what children her age already call "a restaurant game". It is free and lively, but it sells game passes and paid items inside the game, and it never ends. Full Plate does more of the thinking (menus, rotas, money) and has nothing for sale. The second is **Good Pizza, Great Pizza** on the iPad: gentle, funny and rated 4+, but it has adverts and sells gems, and the business side is mostly "earn enough to stay open". The third, for a parent who plays sims, is **Two Point** (the newest, Two Point Museum, is ESRB E) or a classic like **Restaurant Empire II**. Next to those, Full Plate will look smaller and plainer, with fewer things to place and fewer recipes. It is also kinder to a child reader, plays on a tablet in a browser and gives her a real goal at the end. The fair summary: for depth of play it sits nearer the grown-up PC sims than the Roblox and phone games, it is safer than either on spending and age rating, and it has less content than the paid games that have been around for years.
